Equity, as a source of funding, is a lot more expensive than debt. With debt your cost is the interest. With equity, your cost of capital is a portion of all future earnings of the business. Logically, a company should only want to sell equity, or IPO, when the price they can receive is over and above the intrinsic value of the business. 

This can be seen in the long-run performance of IPOs. Professor Jay R. Ritter, of University of Florida, has kept a data set of IPO performance. It's clear that retail investors, buying on the opening day, severely underperformance the broader market over the long term. 

![[LongRunIPOPerformance.webp]]

![[LongRunIPOPerformance2.webp]]

The nuanced take, though, is that some businesses do outperform in the long term. In my personal opinion, it would be biased an unintelligent to completely write off all IPOs, as Graham suggests, because great businesses IPO too. You should just be skeptical, and approach it in the same way that you approach any other business. Understanding the fundamentals, the long term economic prospects, and buying when there is a margin of safety. That might mean buying on opening day, or it might mean waiting years for the price to come down to something more reasonable.
