All systems, including businesses, generate entropy as a byproduct of their own operations. This can be thought of as waste heat, friction, miscommunication, bureaucratic drag, etc. It is a drift towards chaos, and it is a function of the count of nodes in that system, be that cell count, or employees, or some other unit of size. As the system grows, the surface area of the system to the rest of the world squares, while the volume cubes.

As an example, a retail organization's customer-facing surface might loosely be thought of as scaling with a square function, its shops and sales reps generating income for the business. Its central administrative core, by contrast, tends to scale closer to a cube function as the organization grows. As that organization grows, it therefore scales entropy faster than the organization's natural capacity to dissipate or organize it. This is the business world example of the square-cube law playing out.

Innovation is the move that manufactures new surface area internally within the system. These are new channels for information, energy, and order to flow faster and better than the default system allows. Software is an example of this. Instead of information needing to be reported from one person to the next, which requires a meeting, conversation, and understanding, all of which introduce entropy, software allows sales data to be transmitted instantly to the executive decision maker through a data pipeline and dashboard.

This is the business growing elephant ears. It is the "unnatural" extension of surface area within the system. All businesses must do this, not only internally in order to efficiently scale themselves, but also in their product offering to customers. A product or service offering can also be thought of as an opportunity to expand the surface area of the customer. It answers the question of how to help the customer do what they are trying to do better, faster, cheaper, safer. Software might reduce entropy within an organization by allowing information to flow faster and with increased clarity, but a business like Amazon does the same by reducing the time the customer must spend collecting a product, while giving them more choice. Costco similarly reduces entropy through its trust relationship with the customer. It promises not just cost savings, but time savings, as the customer trusts they will get the best price at Costco and thus does not need to shop around.
