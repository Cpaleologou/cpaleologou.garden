# 1. Business Overview

## Core Overview

Booking Holdings (Booking) owns and operates several online travel agencies and other reservation and travel related entities. Its largest brand, Booking.com, headquartered in Amsterdam, lists roughly 4.4 million accommodations across over 220 countries and 40 languages. Booking also owns Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK, and OpenTable, along with several other brands. While brand segment revenue is not reported, Booking.com produces a disproportionate amount of Booking's sales.

**Booking Holdings' Brands and Services**[^1]

![[BookingHoldings_BrandServices.webp]]

At its core, Booking is a two-sided marketplace. On one side are travellers looking for somewhere to stay, and on the other are the hotels and hosts with rooms to fill. Booking earns its money by connecting the two. Its value and durability comes back to how well it serves each side.

The company earns revenues in three ways: [^2]
1. **Agency Revenue** - A direct commission paid by a hotel to Booking *after* the guest stays. The guest pays the hotel directly.
2. **Merchant Revenue** - Booking takes the traveler's payment at the time of reservation, holds it, and pays the hotel later. Booking keeps the margin, plus payment processing fees.
3. **Advertising and other revenue** - These are mostly produced by KAYAK and OpenTable, from sending referrals to partners, advertising placements, restaurant reservation fees, and subscription fees for restaurant management services.

**Booking Holdings Inc., Revenue Types**

![[Booking_Holdings_Inc__Revenue_Types.webp]]

Understanding Merchant and Agency revenue requires knowing two separate terms:
1. Gross Bookings - This is the total purchase value of the transactions, of which most is remitted to the hotel or alternative accommodation partner at check-in.
2. Take-Rate - The take-rate is the commission percentage that Booking charges for the service of connecting the customer and the hotel, processing the transactions, and providing support on either side to ensure a smooth operational, or travel experience.

> **Merchant Revenue can be calculated as:**
> 
> - (a)  Merchant Gross Bookings
> - (b) Take-Rate
>   
>   **Merchant Revenue = (a) x (b)** 

In FY25, the total take-rate, called "% of Total gross bookings", was 14.5%. The increase from the prior year was driven by higher payment processing fees, which come with the shift from Agency to Merchant revenue described below. This is slightly overstated, however, as advertising revenue inflates the picture.

The mix between these types of revenue is important to understand for later sections of the report in terms of assessing business quality, competitive positioning, and durability of the marketplace itself. Over the last ten years, the mix has shifted materially towards merchant revenues. In 2017, merchant revenues were roughly 17% of revenues, while in 2025 they were roughly 66%. The gap has continued to widen into 2026, as seen in the Revenue Types chart above. Why Booking made this shift, and what it gained from it, is the second of the three decisions covered below.

Given the nature of revenues, the atomic unit of the business is a **room night.** Over the trailing twelve months (TTM) to June 2026, Booking sold 1.27 billion room nights on \$197.5 billion of gross bookings. We can then determine that each room night carried about \$155 of gross bookings (including flights and rental cars), and produced approximately \$22.22 of revenue.

## How It Got Here - Three Decisions

### 1. The Origin

Booking.nl, the original site, was founded in 1996 by Geert-Jan Bruinsma, a Dutch final-year university student who noticed that Hilton.com already had an online reservations portal, but could not find a similar one in the Netherlands. He built the Booking.nl site and, out of ignorance, set the business model that later allowed it to win the market against large US corporations: customers paid at the hotels, not on the site, hotels set their own prices, and Booking received only a 5% commission. Bruinsma went door to door in Amsterdam signing up the first hotels. [^7]

From the beginning, site traffic came primarily from search engine advertising. Bruinsma had tried to place an ad in De Telegraaf, the largest Dutch newspaper, but was refused because he wanted to list a web address rather than a phone number. [^7] This dependence on search engines is the subject of the third decision below.

The Booking.nl site was acquired by BookingsPortal in 2003. BookingsPortal was a similar site that ran a "factory" of destination sites for various language travellers. A foreign traveler searching for hotels in a secondary European city would find a site in their native language. The important development at this stage, however, was the intense and systematic A/B testing that the company applied to the landing site.

Booking.com has long been famous for its experimentation and testing culture. You'll often find figures online discussing how they run over 1,000 tests a day, tweaking, moving things around, showing the customer different information. The BookingsPortal era is where this began. The culture created by BookingsPortal is what eventually resulted in a website that converted browsing travellers at a significantly higher rate than the competition, which in turn justified a higher marketing spend and fuelled growth. [^7]

In 2004, The Priceline Group's business development team, led by now CEO Glenn Fogel, purchased a company called ActiveHotels, which was doing a similar thing to Booking.nl, but for independent UK hotels rather than the continental market Booking was going after. About a year later, they purchased Booking and put the two together under the name Booking.com. At the time, each company had about 8,000 hotels on the system with little overlap, so putting the two together greatly expanded the supply of rooms on the sites, thus the value to any single traveller, and thus the value to each hotel on the site individually. [^7] This was the moment of the network effect reaching velocity.

An enormous boots-on-the-ground supply expansion campaign followed from 2005 to 2016. Booking aggressively opened local sales and account management offices, primarily in Europe, but also across the globe. This is important to understand because onboarding the current ~500k hotels was a long, expensive and physical process. It is not something a competitor can execute on a short timeline with access to capital alone. Note that ~500k hotels is far smaller than the 4.4 million accommodations listed today. The difference is alternative accommodation, covered in the next section.

### 2. Shifting To The Merchant Model

The second major decision that built the company was the shift to the Merchant model, which has been happening since around 2017. In the 2000s, the agency model was an advantage. Customers were wary of online payments, so paying at the hotel was preferable and converted at a higher rate. Hotels also preferred it, as they were at the front of the cash flow stream. [^7]

When Fogel was appointed CEO in 2017, he came into the position with a clear strategy called "The Connected Trip". The idea was to expand the marketplace into new verticals like flights, rental cars, taxis, attractions, and restaurant reservations, and let customers create personal bundles of them through the site. The company would then use technology to manage the complexities of travel for the customer, like a traditional travel agent. Examples shared by Fogel include automatically shifting a hotel check-in time and re-booking a dinner reservation after a flight is delayed, moving activities around based on weather, and making personal recommendations based on previous travel history. All of that would be bundled into a strong loyalty program with discounts and other travel privileges. The Connected Trip will come up again in the Moat and Risk & Opportunity sections.

Unlike hotels under the agency model, booking a flight requires paying upfront, and bundling discounts across products requires controlling the price. To build out the vision of The Connected Trip, it was "foundational" for Booking to build out its own payment processing fintech branch. [^8] The service is called "Payments by Booking", and for vendors using it Booking handles payments, cancellation policies, fraud, chargebacks, and local currency payment methods on the vendors' behalf.[^9]

Becoming the merchant of record in a travel transaction also changes Booking's relationship with the customer. It increases the payment options available to customers and puts Booking in charge of customer support, which builds brand trust and direct usage of the product over time.

Booking's merchant model also facilitated another key supply-side growth lever: alternative accommodation. Alt. accommodation became a big focus area for Booking in the 2010s as Airbnb demonstrated the global potential of the market. As of FY25's year end, roughly 89% of the properties on Booking.com were alternative accommodations, though by our atomic unit of room nights they represent only about 36%. This is also what explains the gap between the ~500k hotels and the 4.4 million total properties mentioned earlier. Payments are more significant for alternative accommodations than for hotels. Independent hosts face a lot more challenges than experienced hoteliers in verifying foreign cards, taking foreign exchange gains or losses, or chasing down a chargeback.

The merchant model is also important because it creates float. Customers' money sits on the balance sheet between the time the reservation is made and when the customer checks in, funding the operations of the business. It also earns the business interest income, which, along with rate increases, has given net earnings a meaningful boost in the years since the pandemic. This float shows up again in Section 2, where it is the reason Booking's operating invested capital is negative.

### 3. Capturing Demand

As discussed in the origins section above, Booking.com's traffic has always been highly dependent on search engine advertising. While the main search engine advertised on in the early 2000s was AltaVista, in the 2010s much of Booking's budget shifted to Google. The conversion advantage built by the testing culture meant Booking could afford to pay more for each visitor than its competitors. Today, marketing expense is about 30% of revenues, the single largest cost item on the income statement.

**Booking Holdings' Marketing Expense to Revenue:**[^5]

![[bkng-revenue-vs-marketing-fy18-fy25.webp]]

The problem was that the shift to Google accompanied the tech giant's monopolization of search. With few alternatives, Google set the price of advertising, and therefore the price of every traveller Booking acquired. Google has also made several competitive attempts at hotel booking, flights, etc. throughout the years. [^11] The last decade of Booking's strategy, therefore, has revolved around building a direct-booking channel, separating itself from its dependence on Google and making each booking more profitable as a result.

To see why, it helps to split traffic into paid and direct. A room night bought through paid search earns significantly less than the average, and a room night booked by a customer returning directly to the website or app earns much more, because Booking does not have to pay for that visit again. The lifetime value of a customer, therefore, depends on them coming back.

The first tool to build the direct channel is the mobile app. The second is the Genius program, Booking's loyalty offering. Today the program has three tiers, earned based on the number of bookings made. New accounts are automatically entered into Level 1, while Levels 2 and 3 are achieved by making five and fifteen bookings within two years, respectively. At the time of writing, Level 1 customers get 10% discounts on select stays, while Levels 2 and 3 get 15% and 20% off respectively, along with other benefits.

Both levers are working. As of Q2 FY26, on a trailing twelve-month basis, the B2C direct mix was in the mid-60%'s, mobile app mix was in the high 50%'s, and Genius Level 2 and 3 members accounted for a high-50s percentage of Booking.com's room nights.[^6]

**Booking Holdings FY26 Q2 Key Highlights**[^6]

![[BookingHoldings_EarningPresentation_FY26Q2.webp]]

This is why the direct channel sits at the centre of the current market narrative surrounding the company: will AI agents, used by customers to plan and book their trips, disrupt Booking? If an agent books on the customer's behalf, the customer may never open the Booking app, and the direct relationship built over the last decade could weaken. This will be discussed in detail in the Risk & Opportunity section.

## The Metrics That Matter

With that understanding in place, we can begin to think about the metrics that matter for any owner of Booking Holdings trying to monitor the business's performance.

1. **Room night growth** - As the atomic unit of the business, the quantity of room nights sold represents growth at the fundamental level. While bookings have expanded into flights, attractions, etc., these are complementary, not principal.
2. **Take-rate** - The take-rate represents Booking's pricing power and competitive positioning. Personally, my preference is for this to stay flat. A declining take-rate may indicate a weakening negotiating position, while an increasing take-rate, particularly without value adds to the hotels in return, represents rent-seeking behaviour that appears good in the short term but detracts from the brand's reputation and relationships with its hosts.
3. **Marketing % of Revenue** - Marketing costs represent the cost to acquire new customers.
4. **Direct Mix** - Direct customers are far more profitable than paid customers, for the reasons covered in the third decision above. A shrinking direct mix indicates lower future earnings, while a growing or stable mix, as long as room nights increase, indicates higher future earnings.
5. **Total Properties or Booking Options** - The "moat" of Booking's network will be discussed in further detail below. In short, however, the value of a marketplace is generally derived from the number of vendors and customers. Growing vendor count provides more options for customers, which attracts more customers, which increases the marketplace's value for vendors. One caution is that property count flatters the picture. As shown above, alternative accommodations make up 89% of properties but only about 36% of room nights, so growth in listings does not translate one-for-one into growth in useful supply.

While I typically like to use return on invested capital (ROIC) to judge business quality, for Booking, when you consider that operating invested capital is negative, the ROIC metric as a whole becomes meaningless. ROIC is typically measured as Net Operating Profit After Tax (NOPAT) divided by Operating Invested Capital. Under normal circumstances, it answers the question of how much profit the business makes each year, for each dollar that was historically invested into it by investors. If Operating Invested Capital is negative, the calculation returns a negative number for a business that is highly profitable.

Fundamentally, we don't need ROIC to understand Booking's quality _because_ Operating Invested Capital is negative. This is the float from the merchant model showing up on the balance sheet. Customers, hotels and other suppliers fund the day-to-day operations of the business, not shareholders or lenders (see Appendix B). Growth in Booking's earnings is therefore not dependent on investing more capital, but simply on demand for its service, which simplifies the analysis. The risk with this kind of business is that the float, made up of customer deposits, unwinds if bookings stop growing.

Long-term debt is not an issue either. Debt has risen steadily over the prior periods, which may expose the company to higher interest payments in a period of rising rates. However, when compared against operating cash flow, debt levels have remained flat and completely manageable. An OCF/Total Debt ratio of 0.5 means the entire long-term debt balance could be paid off with two years of organic cash generation.

## Moat Sources

With comfort that some kind of durable competitive advantage exists, we can move on to trying to understand exactly what kind of advantage that is and assess it's durability. To do this, it helps to understand two separate, but related, mental models: Aggregation Theory and Network Effect theory.

### Aggregation Theory

Ben Thompson defined aggregation theory in his newsletter Stratechery in 2015.[^34] The framework divides the value chain of a consumer market into three layers: suppliers, distributors and consumers. Historically the bulk of the profits in that industry went towards companies that were able to either monopolize a full layer, gaining leverage over the others, or integrating two layers, thus gaining an advantage over the competition. Newspapers are his first example. The market dynamic was such that consumers of newspapers far outnumbers the sources of content, and distributing that content to consumers carried a high associated cost in the form of vehicles, fuel, and labour. The most successful newspaper companies owned the distribution capital and then integrated backwards, hiring journalists and writers to simultaneously own the supply the content. With scarce, exclusive content and distribution capabilities, newspapers captured pricing power and captured the value in the industry.

The internet flipped this strategy on its head by changing two key qualities in the value chain. First, the cost of distribution fell to zero. Whether through email or a webpage, getting content and information to a consumer no longer required the vans and labour that it used to. This meant that supply of content and information became commoditized. The entire world of journalists and regular people (as later happened with social media) was available as content to any individual consumer. 

Second, the cost of individual consumer relationships fell to zero. Before the internet, newspapers captured the value in the industry by selling bulk advertising to a geographic group of consumers who *likely* had similar preferences due to their shared location. But through the automation available through code and algorithms, a distributor could learn the needs and preferences of each individual consumer, maintain that relationship across time for near-zero cost, and thus sell hyper specific, and therefore more valuable advertising.

The problem space thus shifted. It was no longer about controlling supply and distribution, it was about helping each individual *find* what they need when they need it. Competition moved forward in the value chain to the consumer space and the company that provided the best user experience won. In the newspaper example, this was, of course, Google.

**Aggregation Theory Illustrated, per Ben Thompson**[^34]![[AggregationTheory_BenThompson.webp]]
What makes Booking particularly interesting, is that it is not a pure front-end aggregator. We saw in Section 1 of this report that roughly 65% of Booking's traffic comes to the site directly, but the remaining 35% actually comes from Google - another aggregator. This is an aggregator above an aggregator situation and it is, again, why direct bookings is such a key number for the business and industry as a whole. It will be discussed at length in the Risks & Opportunities section below.

### Network Effect Theory

The beauty of aggregator businesses - like Google, Facebook, Uber and Booking - is that aggregation also turned out be an extremely powerful virtuous cycle. The best user experience attracts the most users, that mass of users attracts suppliers, more suppliers improve the user experience, which in turn attracts more users. This is a network effect; where each incremental user or supplier increases the value of the network for the next user. The result is an extremely strong and durable competitive advantage that can actually be understood in monetary terms quite easily. 

Let's take another example: Meta (Facebook/Instagram/Whatsapp). 
The technology behind these apps, especially in 2026, is not novel or particularly complicated. A competitor could relatively easily build the same app. But regardless of having the same interface and features, the user would have a much worse experience on the app, simply because no one else is on it. This is a cost to that consumer, and the competitor would have to offset that cost in some other way to convince the user to use their duplicate product instead of Meta's. This is extremely hard to do, especially with Meta's products, because they are already free for the user. 

But, we can imagine a world where a competitor actually pays users to use the alternative app. How much would you have to be paid to use an Instagram-like app instead of Instagram? It is actually likely close to an infinite amount of money because the competitor app is effectively worthless to you as a user unless there are other people using it. It follows that Meta's network effects, derived from their mass of users, is nearly impossible to disrupt because it is simply too expensive for a competitor to do so. 

### Booking Holding's Moat

Booking.com is an aggregator of hotels, alternative accommodations and other travel related activities. The company spent nearly 20 years building out a massive supply base of hotels and alternative accommodations, spending marketing dollars advertising on Google, optimizing its site for conversion rates, and then getting users to come back directly through the mobile app and Genius loyalty program. More properties brought more selection and lower prices, which attracted more travellers, which makes listing on Booking.com more attractive for properties. At some point the network effect cycle started spinning fast enough that Booking created a competitive advantage, allowing it to earn outsized profits to its closest competition.

The same HOTREC study used for the visualizations above also contains data that can help us assess where Booking's supply-side advantage is the deepest. The study, based on 2,713 European hotels, across 28 countries, breaks Booking's share of OTA bookings down by type of hotel. The key findings are as follows:

- Booking holds 71% of OTA bookings at hotels with fewer than 20 rooms.[^42]
- Booking holds 71.8% in towns under 10,000 people.[^42]
- Booking holds 71.6% in alpine regions.[^42]
- Booking holds 69.4% at independent hotels. [^42]

The company's weakest positions sit in the segments of 5-star hotels, chain hotels, and in major urban metropolises. These are the segments Expedia targets. The takeaway is that Booking's lead is largest in the long, fragmented tail of small, independent, leisure and destination hotels, where the company's field organization spent a decade physically onboarding each hotel. The advantage is thinnest in big cities where inventory is concentrated in chains that every OTA, or AI agent can reach.

The second-order insight from the data is that the hotels that depend on Booking the most are also the least digitally equipped. The same study noted that small, independent hotels are underrepresented in AI adoption, or planned AI adoption, and that the same kinds of properties are overrepresented in managing their distribution channels manually, rather than through an automated service.[^39] Furthermore, across all hotel segments, operators report increasing technical complexity to management as their largest issue, rather than commercial challenges.[^41] The point is that the long tail of small hotels rely on Booking, not only for demand aggregation, but also for the technological merchant of record capabilities that come with it.[^40] Booking is no longer just an aggregator for demand, like aggregation theory explains, but also an integrated technological solution for a large part of the wider European accommodation market.

The weakness in the moat comes from two other key factors in network effect theory: Multi-homing and Disintermediation. 

Multi-homing refers to the ability of marketplace users to use a rival at little cost. To understand this, it helps to go back to the Meta example above. We already assessed how a user would require a near infinite amount of money to use a feature-parity instagram app instead of the real instagram app because the value of the service comes from the users, and without them, there is no value. In travel, a hotel faces no such trade-off. Listing on various platforms costs almost nothing and 66% of European hotels use a channel manager to push a hotels rates and availability to every OTA at once from a single source. [^43] 

**How Channel Managers Work (See Link For Animation)**[^44]

![[HowChannelManagersWork.webp]]

This may actually already be appearing in the numbers. The same HOTREC data used above for Booking's 69% OTA marketshare, shows that this percentage has actually fallen over the last 5 years from 71%. My personal opinion is that this is not currently material enough for a conclusion to be drawn, but it is certainly a risk worth monitoring. 

On the demand side multi-homing is just as easy. Travellers often compare prices against other OTAs, Google Hotel Ads and the hotel's own website prior to making a reservation. The AI agent threat discussed below attacks this moat weakness by making comparison easier for the traveller. Booking, however, has two defences against multi-homing. On the demand side, the loyalty program provides value to travellers that is only available on Booking. A level 3 member who books elsewhere forfeits 20% discounts, funded by the hotels themselves, additionally showing the value of Booking's demand volume.[^45] 

The second weakness Booking's network effect faces is disintermediation. This is the risk that the traveller and hotel meet through Booking and then choose to deal directly. Either the traveller finds a hotel on Booking, enjoys their stay and then books directly next time, or a traveller finds a hotel on Booking, and goes directly to hotel on their first visit. If the traveller is willing to spend the time negotiating, a hotel may even be willing to extend the same discount that can be achieved on Booking in order to route around the commission. This again plays into the AI agent narrative where an agent could do this negotiating on your behalf, lowering the time-barrier for travellers. 

The defence for Booking here is the value it is able to add to every booking, so that going direct is worse for one party or the other. Genius discounts are again present, but offering payment solutions is traveller's native currency or payment system is also a large value add that most hotels do not have the technical ability to offer. For a global network like Booking and the travel industry in general, this is incredibly important and helps to explain the shift to Booking's merchant model from section 1 above. Booking also offers customer service in more languages than an independent hotel can offer. AI threat to all of this will be discussed below. Here we are simply trying to understand the where Booking's advantage comes from and why it has persisted over decades. 

It should finally be noted that disintermediation risk does not apply equally to every hotel on Booking.com. We can surmize from the discussion above that the risk is greatest for chains and large hotels with the resources to compete with Booking's value-adds. It is weakest for the independent and small hotelier's that make up the majority of Booking's room nights. As of Q4 2025, the top 10 global chains represented only a low double-digit percentage of total room nights.[^46]

With that understanding formed, we can proceed to understanding the durability of Booking's competitive advantage in a structured way.

# 4. Risk & Opportunity Framework

Like most public corporations, the incentive to avoid lawsuits has resulted in risk sections of the financial statements sprawling far beyond what is actually useful for an owner or prospective investor. That said, based on my read of the financial statements, there are three risks worth discussing that, if materialized, would have a material negative impact to Booking's intrinsic value. These are as follows:

1. The aggregation layer that Booking depends on for new traffic changes. This could be Google increasing the cost of paid clicks, which raises Booking's sales and marketing expenses. Or, a heavier worry for the market currently, AI agents (Muse, Google AI Mode, ChatGPT Dots, etc) become the preferred place for travellers to plan **and book** their travel. If the experience is good enough, this threatens both Booking's indirect, and more importantly, direct booking channel.
2. EU and national regulators restrict Booking sufficiently enough to make it difficult for them to compete with competitors, or restrict the industry enough to hinder the overall tourism market.
3. A travel demand shock, like what happened in 2020, severely impacts Booking's ability to earn, pay down debt, and return earnings to shareholders through their heavy buy-back program. Demand shocks include, but are not limited to, a pandemic, war, or recession. 

The largest opportunity is often the flip-side of the largest risk, simply because both come from the same dearth of foresight. Once the largest risk has been assessed, I will discuss the opportunities for Booking. This discussion will become the bull base in the valuation section.

## Risks

### Risk 1: Disintermediation & Aggregation Disruption

#### AI Agents: What's At Risk

We saw in section 4 of this report that Booking has two main competitive advantages over Expedia. First, its take-rate is higher. Hotels are willing to pay Booking more per room night booked. Second, it spends less on marketing. Two-thirds of Bookings traffic comes direct, and the remaining third converts at a higher rate because of decades of user-interface tweaking and value-added services that Booking offers.[^6] 

There are three ways an AI agent working on a travellers behalf threatens these advantages.

##### A. An Agent That Calls Booking

In this scenario travellers ask their chatbot or agent for help planning their trips, the agent plugs into Booking via a connector and surfaces potential rooms suited to the travellers needs. This already exists in the today's chatbots. You can install the Booking.com connector within the app, ask for a hotel, and get a result that looks like the screenshot below.

**Using Booking's Connector To Find A Hotel In Rome**![[BookingConnectorExample.webp]]

Regardless of how this is monetized by the AI app, it threatens Booking's marketing advantage. In the image above, Claude used Booking because I had the connector installed and asked it to use it. In a world where the customer simply asks for a hotel, its not clear how the app would decide which tool to use. In a future where AI agents truly exist to serve the customer best, one can imagine the agent searching every option before making a suggestion. It is unclear to me, in this world, that Booking's conversion advantage survives that. 

More important, if the agent does use Booking, booking within the AI app threatens the direct-mix that Booking has steady built over the past decade. Customers who used to go straight to Booking, go to their AI first and make the purchase there. 

In announcing Muse, Mark Zuckerberg said that the app would be free for consumers and that Meta would monetize the AI agent by taking a "small fee from transactions"[^47]. If Booking is now required to pay a fee on what were once its most profitable transactions, a large increase in unit volume would be required to offset that. 

##### B. A Low-Fee Agent That Books Directly

In this scenario, rather than use Booking, the agent simply searches the web, or connects to hotel channel managers and books with the hotel directly. The hotel is the merchant of record and, as in the Muse example, the agent takes a fee lower than Booking's current take rate of 12% to 15%.

This is the disintermediation risk discussed in Section 3. Same as today though, it seems more likely to happen with larger hotels and chains that have the technology to integrate with Muse-like AI assistants or optimize for agents using the web.

##### C. An Agent That Finds Hotels On Booking, Or Search, and Books By Email

One of the features that makes AI agents valuable is their ability to connect to your email and calendar and act on your behalf. It seems entirely possible that an agent could visit Booking.com, search for a hotel, use Booking's curated listings and reviews, and then email the hotel directly to negotiate a lower price. When the work of emailing, negotiating and managing the relationship is done for you, even a modest discount may be worth it.

##### What Each Risk Threatens

The three risks do not effect the same advantages. Risk A threatens the demand side; the marketing advantage. Booking's ability to convert paid traffic in to a sale, and then have that user come back directly currently allows them to outbid competitors on search traffic, while making their profit on future bookings. If the direct-mix falls, Booking now pays a fee on customers who used to be free. Risks B and C threaten the supply-side of the network; the take-rate. 

Whether or not these risks come to reality therefore comes down to two questions:
- Risks B & C: What is Booking.com worth to an agent?
- Risk A: How is the value of the service split between Booking and the agent?

#### Game Theory: What Is Likely To Occur

Before spinning off into the many permutations the three risks allow, it makes sense to reason out the likely end state in a world where agentic commerce is common. Below is my own thinking on how this plays out, based on how merchants and agents are behaving today and on my own use of Muse. I have simplified it into five steps: 

1. Agents spread.
2. Merchants decide whether to join.
3. The merchants that join win share. 
4. What Booking is worth to an agent.
5. How that value is split.

**1. Agents Are Helpful And Will Spread**
In my own experience, AI agents are genuinely helpful. I am currently (November 2026) using Muse to help plan and manage my wedding, tutor me in Italian, follow news I'm interested in without opening social media apps, and a host of other things. I have also been testing purchases with Muse. The experience is currently clunky. Sometimes Muse struggles to log into a website, other times it just doesn't have the real-world understanding to find what I want.

For example, I had Muse on voice chat while I measured my closet and asked it to keep a running document of the dimensions, which it did very well. But when I asked it to find dividers and shelving, its suggestions were not what I was looking for. I ended up getting what I needed on Amazon in a regular e-commerce fashion.

Another example genuinely impressed me. I was purchasing a birthday gift for my sister through in-store pick-up, and Muse notice that the product was out of stock at the location I had chosen. However, because it had access to my calendar, it pointed out another location on the way to my friends house which I was going to the next day. I ordered it for pick up at that location and everything went smoothly. That is a real incremental sale for the vendor who might have lost me otherwise. 

So that is to say that the technology is not perfect yet, but, I am starting to understand where it is going and I think the potential is real. Some argue that agentic commerce will never work because large merchants will block agents. I disagree. Agents are useful regardless of whether merchants integrate with them. I get plenty of value from my assistant that has nothing to do with shopping. Merchants that block agents may slow adoption, but consumers have reasons to use these services anyway. Shopping is the bonus feature, and I expect it to improve as the kinks are ironed out.

What will slow agent shopping most is trust in the agentic process itself. When I talk to people today, most are very hesitant to let an AI agent shop for them. I expect this worry to fade as people have useful and safe experiences with their agents, just as worries about making purchases online faded in the 2000s. Getting to that point, however, takes several good experiences, and travel is a hard place to start. Holidays are expensive and bought only a few times a year. Getting the plan or the purchase wrong costs a lot in terms of money and emotion.

**2. Many Agents, Selective Participation**
Not long after Meta announced Muse, OpenAI announced their own version called Dots.[^48] Anthropic then announced Duet[^49]. The personal assistant space is heating up and I expect competition to intensify over the coming years as the main players fight for marketshare.

Simultaneously, merchants will have their finance departments crunch the numbers and determine whether making their website and products available to agents is worth it. How they decide depends on how agent providers choose to monetize. For now, we will lean on what Meta has said for Muse, which is a commission on each transaction. This is actually an important assumption. Whether an agent steers users towards merchants that pay them is economically obvious under a commission model. It would also be likely under an advertising model where merchants bid for an agents attention. Under a subscription model, however, the agent has less reason to favour anyone and is more likely to behave like a rational consumer with infinite time. We will discuss this in more detail below.

In assessing the opportunity of agent integration, each merchant will ultimately weigh two things:

1. The cost of joining - The fee it pays on sales that used to come direct.
2. The benefit of joining - The marketshare it can win from merchants that don't integrate.

A merchant in a dominant market position that also owns its demand would pay a lot and gain little. Why give up 3% on a transaction when you can make the customer come to you for free? These players, the incumbents, will **rationally** block Muse-like agents.

This is already playing out. Amazon blocked Muse from using the site within days of the product's release, which protects the customer relationship and the their advertising business.[^50] Airbnb's founder and CEO, Brian Chesky, has said they are likely to do the same[^51]. 90% of Airbnb's traffic comes direct.[^53]

Merchants with less direct demand are likely to join, simply because they have less to lose and more to win. As we saw, Expedia spends ~\$6.1 of marketing per \$100 of bookings, against Booking's \$4.40. Unsurprisingly, Expedia announced a partnership with Muse near launch day.[^52] 

Booking, somewhere between Expedia and Airbnb, has been candid on their earnings calls: it wants to be where the traveller is and capture the demand.[^55] Agoda, the asia-dominant subsidiary of Booking Holdings, announced a partnership with Muse on October 28, 2026.[^54] Management has also said that they are working on deals across their other platforms. To date though, Booking.com has not joined Muse. With AI chatbots sending well under 1% of current room nights, the cost of waiting is small, and Booking can likely hold out for terms that keep its brand and customer accounts relevant.[^55] 

I suspect we will see more of these go/no-go announcements over the next year, as each business in the digital economy works out where they sit in agentic commerce. 

**3. Participating Merchants Win**
Competing agents need trusted partners to win users. We discussed in step 1 why it is likely that consumers eventually gain trust with their agents in terms of making general purchases. We also noted that travel is a difficult place to start because of the perceived risk for a traveller. This hints at another layer of trust we should discuss: trust in the merchant that your agent deals with.

Here, we can understand the desire for AI agents to sign partnerships with merchants, as well as start to deflect the worries of Risk C above. Brands matter for trust. Seeing your agent scroll the website of a small hotel in Serbia, and email them in a language you don't understand in an attempt to negotiate with them for a lower price is not a high-trust situation. Seeing your agent use Booking.com, or Expedia, or Agoda, with a branded experience within the app, as we can see with Claude above, is a much more trust-friendly experience. If something goes wrong, you know who stands behind the reservation. On the FY26 Q3 earnings call Booking's CFO made the same point: agents want to work with Booking partly because the association gives them instant credibility with travellers.[^56]

The take-away is that agent-users are more likely to shop and complete their transactions when those AI agents have partnerships with trusted brands. The agentic apps need partners to speed adoption and capture consumer marketshare. Simultaneously, AI businesses are expensive and providers need cash flow. If personal assistants are monetized through commission agreements, the agent companies have an incentive to push their agents towards partner vendors, rather than pushing the agents to prefer direct web purchases or email exchanges, even if that is marginally better for the consumer. This begins to weaken likelihood of Risk B and Risk C. Perhaps in the future AI agents integrate their own guarantees and no longer require trusted brand partnerships. But the economics of the relationship still make the partnership path more likely, in my opinion. 

There is a feedback loop beginning to form here. A shopper asks their agent to find a few possible Airbnbs for a trip. The agent is blocked from Airbnb's site and instead shows several alternatives from a partner. Not everyone will leave Airbnb for these options, but some will see a place they like. If that parter is Booking, and the agent books through the traveller's Booking.com account, the stay counts toward Genius, the traveller gets Booking's confirmation, app and customer service, and they have a reason to book with Booking next time. 

As the merchants that join take share, the ones that blocked agents will accept that they need integrations with the most popular agents. The cost of joining and losing direct traffic begins to outweigh the cost of holding out. This will not be a fast process. It took years for many traditional retailers to add e-commerce channels. I expect agent integrations to be faster because no physical infrastructure has to change, but it is still a process of years, not months. We land in a world where agentic commerce is common, consumers tell their agents which retailers they prefer, and consumers with no preference follow the path the agent chooses. 

There are two outstanding questions. When an agent does the booking, what is Booking worth to it? This question decides the take-rate that Booking is able to charge and closes the conclusion on Risks B and C. The second question is how is that value split between Booking and the agent? This decides how much the agent can charge and clarifies Risk A.

But, before we move on, I want to make sure we are all on the same page in terms of what this agentic commerce world for travel actually looks like. Because I think some of you reading are probably pushing back, saying that you enjoy the process of planning a vacation. Browsing the hotels, researching activities, creating an itinerary, etc. This isn't booking a hotel for work-travel. It's fun. You don't want to hand the process off to an agent. I understand that. But, that is not what I am talking about when I say agentic commerce. What I mean by agentic commerce is that you and your agent will collaboratively do that process in some kind of nicely formatted and styled document within the agent app. You will still do the discovery and browsing process, you will just do it within the agent app in natural language, as it does the work of documenting what you want and helping you keep track of everything. When you're ready, and the trip is planned, instead of tediously booking everything individually, you'll just say "book it", and the agent will take care of the rest, in whichever way makes the most sense for you, based on your stated preferences or past behaviour with the agent. Below is an example of what this might look like, and this also touches on another important point: Do consumers, have one super-agent that does everything for them? Or, does every vendor and app have their own agent that we use, similar to how we use apps today?

Amazon has, for example, added its own AI chat bot in their marketplace. Some of Booking's subsidiaries have added similar services.[^57] Booking has also announced new start-up like companies within their group that are "AI-native".[^58] The problem, for me, is that I increasingly believe the world of having "an app for everything" is going away. It seems much more likely that we will have "one app for everything". Or a least, only a few. Here, what I'm doing feels closer to speculating than reasoning. But, put simply, a single app that can use every other app, and has functional long-term memory of what I like, and can bridge that personal context across every job I ask it to complete, feels a lot more value accretive than simply using a chatbot within apps I already use.

##### 4. What Is Booking Worth To An Agent?

Without a doubt, part of the reason a traveller uses Booking.com is because it is more convenient than going to every hotel's individual website, comparing rates, finding them on Google maps, etc. An agent can do that much faster and therefore destroys any advantage Booking has from convenience alone. But we've also discussed the element of trust and why agents need partners. So, lets try to put a number on trust using some basic economics.

Put simply, Booking is worth what a traveller would lose by booking directly, plus the hotel's own cost of handing a direct booking. The cleanest way to see this is in Risk C, when the traveller agrees to the plan and says "book it". The agent has already found the hotel on Booking and read the reviews, so search capabilities and reviews are no longer relevant. The only question is whether the hotel and the traveller would both be better off with a direct booking at a discount.

From the hotel's perspective, the preference is simple. Assume through Booking it pays a commission of about 15%. Direct, it saves the commission but has its own costs - card fees, staff servicing, and some risk to being de-ranked on Booking. We can estimate this at 3-5%. So the furthest the hotel can go on direct discounts is between 10-12% below the price the traveller would pay on Booking. Note that Genius discounts are primarily hotel-funded so it is not relevant to the discussion.[^59]

From the traveller's perspective, they can achieve this direct discount only by giving up the other services that Booking provides, including trust. We will call the discount d, and Booking's value v. The traveller only books direct if the value of d is larger than v. The point where the discount exactly makes up for losing Booking's services is the traveller's indifference curve, and can be visualized as the following.

**Indifference Curve: Booking Direct vs. Using Booking.com**

![[BookingDirect_IndifferenceCurve.webp]]

A direct booking at Booking's price leaves the traveller worse off, because it has the same cost and less service. The hotel has to provide more value before the traveller will switch.

Now, the services provided by Booking in v are not equally valuable for every vacation. As we implied above, Booking provides more value when making a reservation at a small remote hotel in Serbia than reserving a room at the Marriott in Paris. So, if the discount received by the traveller is more than the value provided by Booking, they would go direct. We can get an idea of whether this happens frequently by assigning value to Booking's core offering across three hotel scenarios. The figures below are my own estimates, not sourced or derived.

| Booking's Value                    | Large Chain, Primary City | Medium Hotel, Secondary City | Small Hotel, Alt. Accomodation, Remote City | Reasoning                                                                                                                                    |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Local Currency Payment             | 0.5%                      | 1.5%                         | 2.5%                                        | My personal experience in retail has taught me that the ability to pay in whichever way you want (there are many) is surprisingly important. |

| **Total Estimated Value**          | **2.5%**                  | **9.5%**                     | **14%**                                     |                                                                                                                                              |

Next we can weight these estimated value percentages against Booking's mix to get a weighted average value. 

- Glenn Fogel (CEO) has said in recent earnings calls and at conferences that the ten largest global hotel chains account for only a low double-digit percentage of room nights at Booking.com.[^60] For conservatism, lets estimate that at 15% to account for large chains outside of the top 10.
- We also know that as of Q3 2026, 37% of room nights came from alternative accommodation.[^60] To account for the small and remote hotels where Booking has dominant marketshare, we can round this up to 45%. This is a guess but seems conservative to me.
- That leaves us with 40% remaining room-night mix in the medium sized group.

The weighted average value of Booking's services can thus be estimated as the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Weighted Average Value} &= (2.5\% \times 15\%) + (9.5\% \times 40\%) + (14\% \times 45\%) \\
&= 0.375\% + 3.8\% + 6.3\% \\
&= 10.475\%
\end{aligned}
$$
On these estimates a direct deal works for both sides only between 10.5-12% off the Booking price. At the mid-point, about 11.25% off, each side gains less than 1%. That is a very thin reward for the work and risk of booking direct, even when your AI agent handles everything for you.

If you disagree with me on the relative value of each of Booking's agent surviving offerings, or I'm wildly wrong on Booking's room-night mix, the deal zone widens materially. As it stands, however, the result answers the take-rate question. If agents made this direct negotiation routine, the commission Booking could theoretically keep on an agent booking would trend towards v, plus the hotel's cost of a direct booking, plus whatever charge it can hold onto for discovery. In this model, on a weighted room night mix basis, Booking would keep most of its commission. Where it loses would be concentrated in the large chains where it's value proposition is already weakest. 

Furthermore, as Booking improves it Genius program and adds more value, the gap widens. Agent incentives discussed above, also make direct negotiation less likely. The only question that remains, is how much of that value is split between Booking and the agent.

##### 5. How The Value Is Split Between Booking And The Agent

What we are left with as the highest probability outcome is Risk A. Here, customers plan their trips with their agent, who then uses Booking to complete the purchase. From my perspective, this last step is the hardest to figure out. Depends on a lot of assumptions and possible futures. That said, it is still a matter of negotiation between Booking (and other OTAs) and the agents. Here we will also, finally, consider different agentic business models. 

At the extreme, after negotiation, one or either side walk away, and integration doesn't occur. Here, the agent partners with another OTA or does in fact try to lead the customer toward direct Booking. Based on the analysis performed so far, this does not seem as big of a threat as it first appears. Booking has a wide moat built off its deep supply-side inventory, and demand-side loyalty program and habitual travellers. Since Booking's strength lays in the long tail of fragment medium and small hotels in secondary and tertiary cities, the deal zone is small and Booking likely keeps its demand for quite a while, while agentic commerce grows, acting as an incumbent discussed above.

How competitors there are at the agent layer also matters. A single winner, a dominant super-agent can deploy its weight into charging a lot. This is what Google has done to Booking over the last decade. In a world of competing agents, each agent has less negotiating power against the incumbents. Today, as we have seen, there are multiple well-funded assistants, merchants appear to be integrating with them selectively, and for a consumer, switching between them is cheap. Unlike business activities, most consumer tasks have a short half life, so switching agents is only painful for a brief period while the new agent catches up. Furthermore, it is not difficult to ask my current agent to summarize everything it know and what it is working on to pass off to my new agent. I think these low switching costs keep the agent layer fragmented for longer, and a fragmented agent layer has less leverage. 

Finally, the agentic business model is important. For the past decade, Google has charged Booking for every click that routed a user to its website. Booking pays this whether the visitor books a trip or not. Part of Booking's competitive advantage has been in its ability to convert at a higher rate than its competition, therefore giving it more room to outbid players like Expedia. In an agentic commission model, where every OTA pays a similar rate per booking, Bookings conversion advantage may actually fade away. There is a weird disconnect here in a world where merchants negotiate lower booking fees for themselves, but the agentic app provider is incentivized to push the user towards the merchant with the highest commission fee attached. For this reason, I don't expect this business model to last long. 

It feels much more wholistic for a bidding/advertising model to develop. Here, each player bids what it is willing to pay for an agent's attention. This consolidates incentives between the agent and the merchant and conversion skill becomes relevant again. I am not sure if the ability to convert on a traditional webpage will transition to being able to convert in agentic commerce, but an advantage held for over a decade with constant changes in the consumer, technological and regulatory environment tells me that the conversion skill likely lays in the people and culture of the company, rather than the webpage asset itself. 

In the worse case scenario however, the agent layer consolidates into a single winner, the agent app is able to integrate much of the value Booking provides like customer service and payment guarantees, and pushes Bookings value proposition down towards the value we see in the Large Chain, Primary City scenario above, but for all market segments.

#### Putting It Together

The analysis above can be reduced to three key questions. Asking them in order, and assigning a probability to each, gives us the likelihood of our bear, base, and bull case for the valuation model below.

1. **Do agents become a main way people book travel within ten years?** 
	I put the odds of this at 75%. In Europe, 30% of people still book hotels over phone and email.[^62] Trends do not move as fast as investors and people within the technology industry think. Over ten years, however, I can see a substantial amount of traffic moving to agents. 
2. **If so, does the agent layer consolidate into one super-agent, or stay fragmented?**
	 Technology businesses seems to trend toward monopolies or duopolies (Google Search, Iphone/Android, etc.). This often goes back to network effects discussed in the moat section above. However, on current evidence, I'm inclined to say that to do not see network effects in the personal agent layer. Perhaps there are other forms of competitive advantages that will arise, but to date I am unsure of this. I also think this is a domain that will get more competitive, not less, over the next five years. Currently, we have three main players in Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic. SpaceX also has Grokbot. Google is likely follow with Android integrations, and Apple is likely to continue developing Siri, and specialized agents from Amazon, or Booking themself will likely continue to proliferate for the time being. I will therefore put the odds of this occurring at 50%. 
	 Finally, in this section it is worth discussing what a fragmented agentic layer means for Booking. This is the bull case. The best possible outcome for Booking. Remember, on a weighted basis, between the direct and paid traffic that make up Booking's traveller base, they pay roughly \$4.4 per \$100 of gross bookings on the site. This is the world where their source of paid traffic comes from what is essentially a complete monopoly on search traffic - Google. If the discovery layer for travellers without loyalty to any OTA widens to include several competitors, it allows Booking to spread it's dollars out, find the optimal balance and ultimately reduce its cost. In this world, it is possible that the weighted average transaction made through Booking actually becomes more profitable, not less. 
3. **In both scenarios, where agents consolidate or stay fragmented, does Booking have strong negotiation leverage?**
	Once again, from a travellers perspective, Booking's value proposition comes from its deep inventory of small, independent hotels and alternative accommodations and the company's ability to make booking with them and travelling as a whole easier. From the hotel's perspective, these hotels do not have the time, ability, or funding to get noticed on the internet without aggregating with similar sized hotels across the world on OTA's like Booking, which functions almost like a decentralized union and allows them to compete with the big hotel chains. In a world where agents are incentivized to send traffic to the players that can pay the most for it, it is possible hotels need OTA's like Booking more, not less. My feeling after doing such an analysis is that Booking has leverage. I will put the odds of this at 60%.

## Appendix A - Owner Earnings

Owner Earnings is a concept first put forward by Warren Buffett (to my knowledge) in his 1986 letter to shareholders. The idea is that GAAP earnings, EBITDA, free cash flow, or other earnings-related measures do not fully represent the cash generative ability of a business. To correct this, an investor can calculate "Owner Earnings", in other words the excess cash a business generates that can either be reinvested into growth or returned to the owners of the business. Fundamentally, it answers the question: how much would this business earn if it stopped trying to grow, and only tried to hold its current market share?

> [!note] Owner Earnings Adjustments
> Owner Earnings is calculated from the following components:
>
> - (a) Reported earnings
> - (b) Normalizing adjustments
> - (c) Depreciation, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges
> - (d) The average annual amount of capitalized expenditure for plant and equipment that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and unit volume
> - (e) Any increase in working capital required to hold that position and volume
>
> **Owner Earnings = (a) + (b) + (c) − (d) − (e)**

**Material Adjustments Explained:**
1. **Remove Non-Operating Items** - Equity and foreign exchange gains/losses are accounting measures, not operating.
2. **Transformation Costs** - Management is undergoing a large multi-period transformation program that they expect to materially lower future operating costs.
3. **Legal/Regulatory/Tax items** - These are complex expenses and gains that vary in terms of materiality and nature year-to-year. Booking removes them from their own adjusted measures. However, they are real economic costs and appear each year. I adjust for the 4-year average rather than ignoring them completely.
4. **Relationship Building Marketing** - Booking's largest cost item is marketing. The large majority of this comes from performance marketing, primarily on Google and social media.[^22] Simultaneously, the share of total room nights booked by customers coming directly to Booking.com was a mid-fifties percentage in 2025.[^23] We can conclude that some portion of performance marketing creates a customer relationship asset, where that customer returns directly for their next booking needs. This is not represented in GAAP standards, but is a true economic benefit of marketing expense. For the adjustment above, we assume that 90% of Booking's marketing expense goes towards performance marketing, and that 20% of that spending creates a customer relationship asset. We also assume that relationship has a 3-year useful life. Thus, for each of the past three years, we capitalize 20% of Booking's performance marketing, and then amortize it over the assumed useful life. A similar exercise is performed on the remaining 10% of marketing costs spent on brand marketing. Full details can be found in the worksheet linked.[^24]
5. **Intangible Amortization and KAYAK Impairment Charges** - Again, these are accounting charges. Amortization of acquired intangibles and impairment charges are relics of cash that was paid several years ago.
6. **D&A and Maintenance Cap. Ex.** - This is slightly pedantic for a low cap-ex demand business like Booking. But for completeness, we assume the capital expenditure required for Booking to maintain its market position is the same as depreciation and amortization. The two adjustments net to zero.

## Appendix B - Operating Invested Capital

Operating invested capital is used to understand *who* funds the day-to-day operations of the business. By netting the operating assets against the operating liabilities of Booking Holdings, we get -\$4.6B. This tells us that the operations of the business are funded by the suppliers, merchants and customers, plus extra (float). Shareholders and lenders are not needed to finance operations.
Goodwill is included in the calculation because it represents the premium paid for acquisitions over the assessed value of the tangible assets. Including it helps quantify management's capital allocation ability. In this case, the operating invested capital is negative regardless of whether goodwill is included or not.
