## Takeaways

The P/E ratio of Amazon has been above thirty since 2006. 

![[amzn_pe_ratio.webp]]

When you include years with near-zero or negative earnings, the ratio becomes even more extreme. In fact, versus the S&P500, Amazon's average valuation has been about ten times more expensive than the index for thirty years.

![[WhereTheMoneyIs_AmazonValuationVsSnP500.webp]]

For most value investors, ever looking for a bargain, or at least a fair price, the stock has always looked like one to avoid. Other metrics favoured by so called "intelligent investors" have told a similar story. GAAP profits and returns on invested capital have looked negative many times in Amazon's history. Yet, since 2006, Amazon is up ~12,600%, while the S&P500 is up only ~440%. 

![[amzn_vs_sp500_returns.webp]]

With two decades of underperformance behind them, any honest Warren Buffett disciple  has to ask themselves where they went wrong. After all, Jeff Bezos himself is a massive fan of Buffett and frequently discussed concepts familiar to value investors in his annual letter to shareholders. With hindsight, it is perfectly clear that Amazon was a value play all along, and Bezos, along with his investors, reaped massive reward. 

But how did they know? Sophisticated investors don't gamble. They don't just throw money to the next new technology, or hot stock. They are thoughtful and systematic. The problem is that the traditional system taught to investors by Benjamin Graham, Buffet and Munger, didn't work for one of the best companies that has ever existed. Is this the end of value investing, or, is it something else? Is it the end of the discipline that has brought so many successful investors enormous wealth, or is it that our toolbox just needs an overhaul?  

This is the question Adam Seessel raises in his book *Where The Money Is*, and he suspects the answer is the later. After all, Bezos understands value and how to measure a businesses performance. So how did he so successfully steer the ship of Amazon for thirty years despite many of the common indicators flashing red? What can investors do, seeking to keep the core tenants of value investing alive, in order to thrive in what has come to be known as value 3.0, value investing in the digital age?

### Why Value 2.0 Broke

Ben Graham founded the concept of value investing in the first half of the twentieth century. The idea was simple - buy securities for less than they are worth. He devised a quantitative, reproducible system for getting rich in the stock market by hunting for the ugliest, most hated businesses, that were trading at a price less than the value of their net assets. He figured that this was as clear of a mispricing as you could get and effectively impossible to lose money investing in. He was right, and he and his disciples, for a long time were able to grind-out profits in the market by buying what Warren Buffett calls "cigar butt" companies. These are businesses that are effectively one-hit profit producers. You buy them for less than they are worth and you sell them when the market inevitably reprices them to what they are worth. 

Buffett himself, the high-priest of Value 2.0, got rich doing this too. However, at some point, the technique stopped working so well. It was a difficult method to scale because  it was a challenging and tedious process. Every successful trade produces a little bit of profit and had no lasting wealth driving effects beyond the trade itself. Once you made enough money from the methodology, there simply weren't enough opportunities  to deploy capital efficiently and to keep it growing. Simultaneously, as more people entered the market and technology made information more abundant, the market became more efficient. You'd be hard pressed to find many cigar butt opportunities today. 

In the early 70's, under the influence of Charlie Munger, Buffett realized there was another way to invest. A better way. It was still rigorous and disciplined, but it also solved the issues with Ben Graham's approach. In Buffett's words, they started buying wonderful businesses at fair prices instead. What he meant by this was that some businesses, like See's Candy, Coca-cola, or Geico could take in so much more invested capital, and through excellent unit economics, growth prospects, and qualities that made their competitive advantage extremely durable, they would earn back their higher valuations relatively quickly. From there, the rest of their growth and cash generation was pure profit for the owner. 

The world had changed, and the methodology an intelligent investor had to change too. Buffett became, at one point, the richest person in the world. Yet, the foundational principles taught by Ben Graham remained: 
- The market exists to serve you, not to guide you.
- Buying a stock is buying a business, not a lottery ticket.
- Applying a margin of safety protects you from being wrong.

This was still value investing. This new approach was still buying a business for less than its worth. It's just that the tools, thinking patterns and perspective an investor used shifted. This is what has become known as Value 2.0.

Nonetheless, the world doesn't stop changing. Value 2.0 produced unfathomable wealth for investors of Berkshire, and spawned multiple generations of enlightened students of Buffett and Munger.
