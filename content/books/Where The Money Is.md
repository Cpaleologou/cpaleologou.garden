## Summary

Value investor's have missed too many of the best business of the 21st century and a change is required to our toolset to see why. By understanding the advantages of digital business, their ability to scale globally with speed and minimal capital investment, produces GAAP induce illusions on the income statement that our valuations need to take into account. If an investor makes these adjustments in a thoughtful and rigorous manner, they can quantify the advantages of digital businesses and more clearly and reliably find those that are undervalued by the market.

---

## Takeaways

The P/E ratio of Amazon has been above thirty since 2006. 

![[amzn_pe_ratio.webp]]

When you include years with near-zero or negative earnings, the ratio becomes even more extreme. In fact, versus the S&P500, Amazon's average valuation has been about ten times more expensive than the index for thirty years.

![[WhereTheMoneyIs_AmazonValuationVsSnP500.webp]]

For most value investors, ever looking for a bargain, or at least a fair price, the stock has always looked like one to avoid. Other metrics favoured by so called "intelligent investors" have told a similar story. GAAP profits and returns on invested capital have looked negative many times in Amazon's history. Yet, since 2006, Amazon is up ~12,600%, while the S&P500 is up only ~440%. 

![[amzn_vs_sp500_returns.webp]]

With two decades of underperformance behind them, any honest Warren Buffett disciple  has to ask themselves where they went wrong. After all, Jeff Bezos himself is a massive fan of Buffett and frequently discussed concepts familiar to value investors in his annual letter to shareholders. With hindsight, it is perfectly clear that Amazon was a value play all along, and Bezos, along with those who saw it reaped massive reward.

But how did they know? Sophisticated investors don't gamble. They don't just throw money to the next new technology, or hot stock. They are thoughtful and systematic. The problem is that the traditional system taught to investors by Benjamin Graham, Buffet and Munger, didn't work for one of the best companies that has ever existed. It didn't work for Google or Microsoft either. In fact, most digital businesses have looked too expensive for value investors their entire lives, while they have continued to carry much of the markets growth. 

Is this the end of value investing, or, is it something else? Is it the end of the discipline that has brought so many successful investors enormous wealth, or is it that our toolbox just needs an overhaul?  

This is the question Adam Seessel raises in his book *Where The Money Is*, and he suspects the answer is the later. After all, Bezos understands value and how to measure a businesses performance. So how did he so successfully steer the ship of Amazon for thirty years despite many of the common indicators flashing red? What can investors do, seeking to keep the core tenants of value investing alive, in order to thrive in what has come to be known as value 3.0, value investing in the digital age?

### Why Value 2.0 Broke

Ben Graham founded the concept of value investing in the first half of the twentieth century. The idea was simple - buy securities for less than they are worth. He devised a quantitative, reproducible system for getting rich in the stock market by hunting for the ugliest, most hated businesses that were trading at prices less than the value of their net assets. He figured that this was as clear of a mispricing as you could get and near impossible to lose money on. He was right, and he and his disciples, for a long time were able to grind-out profits in the market by buying what Warren Buffett calls "cigar butt" companies. These are businesses that are effectively one-hit profit producers. You buy them for less than they are worth and you sell them when the market inevitably reprices. 

Buffett himself, the high-priest of Value 2.0, got rich doing this as well. However, at some point, the technique stopped working. It was a difficult method to scale because  it was a challenging and tedious process. Every successful trade produces a little bit of profit and had no lasting wealth enhancing effects beyond the trade itself. Once you made enough money from the methodology, there simply weren't enough additional opportunities to deploy capital into and your money keep it growing. Simultaneously, as more people entered the market and technology made information more abundant, the market became more efficient. You'd be hard pressed to find many cigar butt opportunities today. 

In the early 70's, under the influence of Charlie Munger, Buffett realized there was another way to invest. A better way. It was still rigorous and disciplined, but it also solved the issues with Ben Graham's approach. In Buffett's words, instead of finding cigar butts, they started buying wonderful businesses at fair prices. What he meant by this was that some businesses, like See's Candy, Coca-cola, or Geico could take in so much more invested capital, and through excellent unit economics, growth prospects, and qualities that made their competitive advantage extremely durable, they would earn back their higher valuations relatively quickly. From there, the rest of their growth and cash generation was pure profit for the owner. 

The world had changed, and the methodology an intelligent investor used had to change too. With his new framework, Buffett became, at one point, the richest person in the world. Yet, the foundational principles taught by Ben Graham remained: 

- The market exists to serve you, not to guide you.
- Buying a stock is buying a business, not a lottery ticket.
- Applying a margin of safety protects you from being wrong.

This was still value investing. This new approach was still buying a business for less than its worth. It was just measuring opportunities with different tools. This is what has become known as Value 2.0. An entire essay, or book could be written on the methodology, however, in short, it consisted of qualitatively assessing the competitive advantages a business has, its growth prospects, management's ability, and then paying a low price-earnings ratio for it at an opportune time. 

Value 2.0 produced unfathomable wealth for the shareholders of Berkshire. Buffett and Munger spawned multiple generations of enlightened students as well. Nonetheless, the world doesn't stop changing. Berkshire's compounded returns have dwindled closer to the returns of the index since the late 90s. Is it a coincidence that this was almost the exact time period that Bezos moved his family to Seattle and started selling books from his garage on Amazon.com?

> **\$1,000 USD invested in Berkshire Hathaway in 1964 would be worth \$61 million today. The same \$1,000 USD invested in the S&P500, dividends reinvested, would be worth only \$462 thousand.**
>![[berkshire_vs_sp500.webp]]
>
>You can see the massive outperformance begin in the mid 70's. You can also see the outperformance tail off with the rise of the digital age in the late 1990s.

When the dot-com bubble burst in '99, news outlets were fast to declare the internet as a failed experiment, a fad. Barron's Jacqueline Doherty famously declared Amazon.com as Amazon.bomb[^1]. I won't retell the obvious here. Amazon was clearly not the blow up the stock market thought. The internet did change the world just as the market predicted in 1998. It just took a lot longer than everyone thought.

Wise as ever, Buffett and Munger avoided much of the internet-driven frenzy and subsequent pain of the dot-com era. Sticking to his core principle of only investing in his 'circle of competence', Buffett claimed ignorance to technology and happily stuck to the businesses he felt he could accurately assess the long-term value of. If he was going to buy a technology company, like Berkshire's massive investment in Apple in 2012, it would be at "fair" multiples. In Apple's case that was around 12x earnings. 

Yet, as mentioned several times throughout this summary, Amazon, in 2012 trading for over 100x earnings, was still a value play! Bezos knew that. Many of his investors knew that. And fundamentally, Amazon was not an extremely difficult business to understand, like say, semiconductor manufacturing. 

In fact, Amazon is not the only value play that Buffet and his disciples missed. Buffett and Munger have repeated kicked themselves for missing Google, a company that came across their desks when Geico was paying for advertising on the platform in the 2000s. In Buffetts words, anytime you're getting paid for people to just click a little button on their desk, you have a great business. 

So in fact, these businesses were perhaps not as hard to understand as claimed. That was not the real issue. And Amazon particularly had no issue with management either. The problem was price. Internet and technology businesses have almost always looked too expensive on a P/E basis. But, what Adam Seessel argues in the book Where The Money Is, is that this tool is broken. The internet changed the world, and the accounting rules haven't caught up. There are qualities that digital businesses have that have never existed before and under the current set of accounting standards, make these businesses look much more expensive than they really are. Investors in the 21st century have to change, like Buffett in the 70s, and incorporate their understanding of the new world into the valuation tools they use to understand the price of the businesses they are looking at. This is value 3.0. 

### Value 3.0

In his 1986 letter to shareholders, Buffett defined a concept he called "Owner's Earnings". Berkshire had purchased a business called Scott Fetzer, and Buffet wanted to show his shareholders how the accounting rules made the acquisition look worse than it was in reality. Avoiding the nuances of accounting, in short, Scott Fetzer's standalone financial statements showed higher earnings than Berkshire's consolidated statements. The same business, in the same year, different numbers. "Owner's Earnings", as Buffett described it, adjusted the GAAP earnings to achieve a number that better represented the amount of excess earnings the business actually produced, and that could be used to value the business fairly. 

> [!note] Owner Earnings Adjustments
> Owner Earnings is calculated from the following components:
>
> - (a) Reported earnings
> - (b) Normalizing adjustments
> - (c) Depreciation, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges
> - (d) The average annual amount of capitalized expenditure for plant and equipment that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and unit volume
> - (e) Any increase in working capital required to hold that position and volume
>
> **Owner Earnings = (a) + (b) + (c) − (d) − (e)**

Understand what Buffett is doing here. He is adjusting the income statement of the company to see how much cash the business produced if it stopped trying to grow and just stayed in place. Most of those adjustments came through understanding capital expenditure.

Value 3.0, as described by Seessel, is the same concept. We want to achieve owner's earnings for digital businesses. The difference, is that digital businesses, don't have material capital expenditure costs to weigh them down. This is the first part of what has led so many value investors astray, missing businesses like Google. Making the same adjustments Buffett made to Google in the 2000s would't have changed the perspective of Google's earnings very much, and as a result, it would have still looked expensive. 

What value investors missed was that the nature of digital businesses allows them to grow rapidly and go global much faster than any kind of business before them. Software companies, like Microsoft, could launch in America and Europe on the same day with almost no extra work or spending required. This means that the money they are saving on cap. ex., can be instead allocated to marketing and sales, accelerating their growth. What's more is that customer acquisition is actually a long term relationship. It's not the same as a shopper grabbing a can of soup off a shelf. Software is often a pseudo-necessary tool for them to live or work, and users usually use digital products for years. Conceptually then, some portion of sales and marketing costs create an intangible asset with a life of multiple years, which should be capitalized to the balance sheet, rather than expensed immediately as GAAP rules demand. Depending on the business, this alone can make a business appear much cheaper than it really is. 

And finally, for many digital businesses, they spend constantly on research and development to improve their products. GAAP accounting requires this to also be expensed immediately rather than capitalized. But for businesses with proven product-market fit, this spending is generally a lot closer to development than research. This is, for example, the cost Adobe spends to add a new feature to its product suite. 

In the book Seessel walks through the example below, adjusting Intuit, the company behind Turbotax and Quickbooks, to look like a similar sized mature company like Campbell's, arriving a mature valuation much cheaper than Intuit looked at the time the book was published.

![[WhereTheMoneyIs_IntuitAdjustments.webp]]

I have to add that I slightly disagree with Seessel here. The tech industry, software particularly, is extremely competitive. The brilliant economics of software is not confidential information. Everyone knows it is the best business model. I think it is therefore naive to assume Intuit's R&D expense could ever look like that of Campbell's. Perhaps the digital and physical nature of the industry requires ongoing development to stay competitive. I think, just as Buffett described the difference between maintenance cap. ex. and growth cap. ex. we should consider the idea that digital businesses require maintenance R&D representing the security updates, data maintenance, and even new feature development required not to lose ground to new products. Furthermore, while I think this has always been true, it is even more clear with the recent "Saaspocalypse" spurred by fears around AI disruption. How much R&D do these businesses need to spend going forward to maintain a competitive feature set against new AI built businesses and competitors, and will this dance ever end beyond the era of AI? I doubt it. So, if we truly want to get to the bottom of how much free cash the business produce, just to hold it's marketshare, some part of this R&D needs to be capitalized, but not all of it. 

I also have to disagree with Seessel on his Amazon point. Amazon, between building out its network of warehouses, vehicle fleets, or physical stores is almost closer to a traditional value 2.0 business than a digital business in many ways. Amazon, being a curated culture of innovators, has spent significant amounts of shareholder capital on initiatives that failed, re. the Amazon Fire phone. That's okay for most shareholders - you know your paying for innovation and failure when you buy Amazon - but its flatly incorrect to wash away all of Amazon's R&D because it is "digital". 
The exception being AWS, or Amazon Web Services, which Amazon invented in 2002 and then faced nearly 7 years without competition, building out and establishing a lead in what turned out to be a trillion dollar industry and its largest business segment. 

![[Amazon2026_Business Segments.webp]]

So Seessel's main point is that physical businesses pass growth spending through the balance sheet, but digital businesses pass growth spending through the income statement, and value investors need to make adjustments for it, I completely agree with. But, I simply add the cautionary point that the adjustments an analyst should make are not as simple as Seessel makes it seem in the book, and are frankly overly aggressive. Adjustments need to be made thoughtfully and after truly studying and understanding each business. Nonetheless, the broader point is extremely valuable and the entire framework laid out by Seessel is worth knowing.

### The BMP Framework

Seessel's BMP framework stands for Business-Management-Price. These are effectively the same things Buffett looked for in value 2.0, but structured in a checklist style worksheet that any analyst can follow.

[[Adam Seessel's BMP Framework (Expanded)]]

#### Business Quality

The first task for the analyst is to understand the business. Particularly, the analyst needs to understand whether the business is growing, and how long of a run way it has in front of it. I've added some subquestions to Seessel's question to help answer. Note, that answers to these questions may or may not be provided by management in their disclosures, investor presentations, or earning calls. Regardless, you should treat management's assessment of the questions with a grain of salt and use your own judgement, to decide your opinion.

- [ ] **Does the company have a low market share of a fast and growing market? (Yes/No)**
	- How big is the TAM (total addressable market)?
	- How fast is the market growing?
	- Is the market growing because of demographic or secular trends, or is it cyclical and likely to reverse?
	- What is the company's estimated marketshare?
	- Is the company's marketshare growing? I.e. Is revenue growing faster than the market?

The second question relates to the durability of the business over decades. All businesses die at some point. If you are performing a discounted cash flow, this stage is effectively assessing the terminal growth rate of the business, which importantly, represents often between 40-70% of a businesses worth.

- [ ] **Does the business have a sustainable competitive advantage i.e. moat? (Yes/No)**
	- Does the business have network effects, i.e. do more users directly make the product more valuable to each user?
	- Does the business produce switching costs for the customer, i.e. is the pain of switching to a competitor more painful than the benefits a competitor offers?
	- Does the business have cost advantages, i.e., access to resources, scale economics, or geographic advantages.
	- Does the business have intangible assets with long lived characteristics i.e., brand, patents, licenses, or proprietary data
	- Does the industry have efficient scale benefits. Some industries are just too small for multiple players to opporate, but can be profitable for one business to serve.
	- Does counter-positioning exist? This is where competitors can see the threat a business produces, but cannot respond without jeopardizing their own existing business. 

There are more types of moats than this, but these are some of the most common. Note that if you are looking for a moat, it can be easy to find one that doesn't actually exist. My advice is to instead, go through each type of moat, and try to *disprove* it, rather than prove it. That usually provides a more honest outcome than the alternative. In the words of Munger - Invert, always invert.

#### Management Quality

The second segment of Seessel's framework is to assess the quality of management. As I get more experienced, and think more about businesses and investing, I become increasingly focused on this segment as the most important part. Ultimately, was are looking for CEOs and management teams that understand what drives business value over the long term - that is, cash flow, not revenue growth or GAAP earnings, or "adjusted earnings", or EBITDA. 

- [ ] **Do the executives understand what drives business value? (Yes/No)**
	- Does management measure and report metrics that map to true economic earnings, rather than metrics chosen because they flatter the number?
	- Does management talk about ROIC, durability, and reinvestment opportunity in a way that shows they understand where value comes from?
	- Do actual historical decisions follow what is discuss in the above questions?
	- How do managers behave in their day-to-day activities? For example, do they make business trips alone, or with a full team? Do they fly first class and stay at fancy hotels, bought on the company card, or, if they do these things, are the upgrade bought with their own money? 

Return on capital is the most important metric for any business. You'd be surprised how many CEO's fail to understand this. Other's will say all the right things and act in an opposite manner. The best way to assess how manage thinks and acts is to study their past behaviour.

- [ ] **Does management think and act like owners? (Yes/No)**
	- Does management hold meaningful personal equity, ideally bought with their own cash rather than just granted?
	- Is capital returned or deployed on terms a rational owner would accept? (buybacks bought at lows, disciplined deal pricing)
	- Is compensation tied to per-share value creation rather than size, revenue, or adjusted profit metrics that are easy to inflate? 

Here we are assessing the alignment between what shareholders want and what management wants. Compensation plans and proxy statements are often exceedingly long and convoluted. What matters is that you as the investor understands what drive business value and that management is incentivized to achieve the same outcome.

One other important I want to make here is that you will often find great businesses with strong moats and as a result, pricing power. Some businesses though will abuse this position, frequently raising prices and squeezing money out of their customers without offering any improvements to their product or service in return. This is a management choice, and while it will look good in the short term as margins expand, it is often destructive to value in the long term. We want businesses with product or service offering that are beloved by their customers, that they happily hand their money over for, not resented. 

#### Price

Price is the final component of Seessel's framework. For him it is the most important and has veto power. That is, if his adjusted valuation does not result in at least a 5% yield (this is equal to a P/E ratio of 20x), he passes, perhaps adding the business to his watch list. One thing to point out is that since this book was published, 10-year US treasury yields and as a result interest rates, have risen considerably. Without getting into the technical details, investors can now get a better return with less risk than they would take on in common stocks. In turn, theoretically, this is raise the yield (or lower the P/E ratio) required for the framework.

Seessel's pricing process works in effectively 5 steps:

1. Start with 2 or 3 year forward revenue. 
	As discussed above, these businesses are able to grow so much faster than physical businesses. If you're trying to assess the ability for a business to earn in the future, trailing revenues punishes the business valuation in a meaningful way.
2. Make standard adjustments to one-time costs, maintenance cap. ex. future costs found in the balance sheet, as described in detail above.
3. Break the business down by reported segments, as much as possible. This allows for more precise adjustments in the next step.
4. Try to estimate segment operating margins by finding comparable businesses. For example, using Ebay to estimate Amazon's marketplace margins. 
5. Assess the income statement for line items where GAAP obscures owner earnings. For tech businesses this is often with R&D and Sales and Marketing expenses. 
	I've provided my opinion on this above, but the extent to which an analyst capitalizes or removes these costs is at their own judgement. 

### Patience Is Still The Discipline

This is an excellent book and I recommend it to anyone interested after reading this. Yet, I do believe Seessel is overly aggressive in his framework. Any student of Ben Graham will know that one of the most important principals to investing is using a margin of safety - applying a gap between your honest valuation and your purchase price - to protect your downside risk. I don't feel Seessel does that. In fact, in many cases it seems he does the opposite, adjusting the earnings of these tech businesses to justify the high cost rather than honestly assess it. I also think there some judgemental gaps as discussed above, such as his neglect for what I've called "maintenance R&D". Seessel's Framework is nice because it focuses on what matters and solves a particular mistake that value investors have made for many years. But it's also fast. And unlike business, in investing speed is not necessarily an advantage. Missing an opportunity, missing Amazon, is okay. We are riding the exponential curve of economic growth.There are and will be more opportunities in the market. We can certainly learn from the mistakes of missing Amazon or Google, but what's more important, is not losing money. Staying in the game is more important. Staying on the exponential. There is no rush. There are no rules. Desperation leads to failure. Patience is still the discipline, and that hasn't changed since Ben Graham endowed us with his teachings. I'll end with a passage from another book - *Zen and The Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* by Robert Persig, described by him as he and his son journeyed through the Rocky Mountains.

> *Mountains should be climbed with as little effort as possible and without desire. The reality of your own nature should determine the speed. If you become restless, speed up. If you become winded, slow down. You climb the mountain in an equilibrium between restlessness and exhaustion. Then, when you’re no longer thinking ahead, each footstep isn’t just a means to an end but a unique event in itself. This leaf has jagged edges. This rock looks loose. From this place the snow is less visible, even though closer. These are things you should notice anyway. To live only for some future goal is shallow. It’s the sides of the mountain which sustain life, not the top. Here’s where things grow.*
